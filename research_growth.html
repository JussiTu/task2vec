Growing, Coasting, or Stuck:
Engineer Career Trajectories in Open-Source Data

Apache Camel, Spark & Hadoop 378 engineers · 45,000 tickets · 15 years

We linked 45,631 Jira tickets to their git commits across three major Apache projects and tracked how the complexity of each engineer's work changed over their career. The results challenge a core assumption of the 70-20-10 learning model: that on-the-job experience automatically builds capability. Only 11% of engineers showed measurable growth — taking on progressively harder, broader, more cross-cutting work over time. 20% were coasting — drifting toward simpler work and slowing down even as the tasks got easier. 70% showed no measurable trajectory in either direction. The signals that distinguish these groups are visible years before they become apparent to managers — and they live in data every engineering team already has.

Dataset

We used the publicly available Jira archives and git histories of three Apache Foundation projects: Apache Camel (integration framework), Apache Spark (distributed computing), and Apache Hadoop (distributed storage). All three maintain a convention of referencing Jira ticket keys in commit messages, enabling systematic linkage between ticket metadata and code change records.

Bare-clone repositories were fetched with --filter=blob:none to minimise download size. Commits were scanned for PROJECT-#### patterns using a regex, and the matched files were extracted via git diff-tree. Only resolved tickets with at least one matched commit were included in the career trajectory analysis.

Complexity Measurement

Each linked ticket was assigned a complexity score based on the files changed in its associated commits. Three dimensions were combined into a single normalised score:

complexity = 0.4 × n_modules + 0.3 × log(n_files + 1) + 0.3 × core_pct × 5
# then normalised 0–1 across all tickets in the dataset

n_modules counts how many distinct software modules were touched (e.g. camel-core, camel-aws, spark/sql, hadoop-common). n_files is the raw file count — log-scaled to reduce the influence of bulk commits. core_pct is the fraction of changed files belonging to the project's core modules (defined per project: Camel's camel-core/api/support cluster, Spark's core and common, Hadoop's hadoop-common and hadoop-auth).

Core modules were identified by examining which modules appear most frequently across high-watch, slow-resolving tickets — a proxy for architectural centrality. Tickets touching these files were weighted more heavily, reflecting the judgment that changing core infrastructure is genuinely harder than peripheral changes.

High-complexity tickets (top quartile by score) attracted an average of 5 comments vs. 2 for low-complexity tickets, and resolved in 8 days vs. 2 — suggesting the complexity score captures genuine difficulty, not just code volume.

Career Arc Analysis

For each engineer with 10 or more linked tickets, we ordered their tickets chronologically and computed a rolling complexity score. We then fit a Spearman rank correlation between ticket index (chronological position) and complexity score to detect the direction of their career arc:

rho_complexity = Spearman(ticket_index, complexity_score)
rho_velocity = Spearman(ticket_index, resolution_days)
# rho_complexity > +0.2 = growing complexity over career # rho_velocity < -0.2 = getting faster over career

A second correlation tracked velocity — whether resolution times improved over the career, independently of complexity. The combination of these two signals defines the three trajectories.

Work experience had no consistent impact on the complexity they could handle or their speed at handling it. The 70% assumption of the L&D model — that doing the job teaches you what you need — did not hold.
Why this matters for the 70-20-10 model. The corporate learning framework assumes that 70% of professional development happens through on-the-job experience. Our data suggests this assumption is optimistic: only 11% of engineers showed the complexity and velocity trajectory that indicates genuine skill development. The 70% either plateaued or drifted. Experience is necessary but not sufficient — the right experience is what drives growth.

What the Growing 11% Actually Did

Growing engineers didn't simply receive harder tickets. Their trajectories show a consistent three-stage pattern that we refer to as the escalation arc:

1
Repetitions in one domain
The engineer works on similar ticket types within a single module cluster. Resolution times begin to decline — pattern recognition accumulating. This phase is visible as a tight, downward velocity curve within a narrow semantic cluster.
2
Velocity improves, scope expands simultaneously
Tickets take less time AND touch more files and modules. Getting faster while handling broader scope is the transfer signal — evidence that mastery in one area is generalising outward. This phase typically lasts 1–3 years.
3
Complexity ceiling rises
Cross-cutting problems, core module involvement, higher watcher counts per ticket. The work itself demanded more, and the engineer met the demand. 9 engineers in our dataset (2.4%) reached full mastery: rising complexity with simultaneously improving velocity across the whole career arc.
The escalation arc is detectable before the advancement happens. The velocity improvement in phase 2 typically precedes a measurable complexity ceiling rise by 6–18 months. This means a manager with access to ticket and commit data could identify engineers approaching a readiness threshold before promotion decisions are made — not after.

What the Coasting 20% Looks Like

Coasting engineers are the most operationally invisible group. Standard engineering metrics — tickets closed per sprint, pull request frequency, code review participation — show no signal. The coasting pattern only becomes visible when you track the complexity content of the work, not its volume.

Coasting engineers are productive contributors who are quietly de-skilling. The typical coasting trajectory involves 3–5 years of gradually narrowing ticket scope before the pattern becomes apparent even to the engineer themselves. By the time a capability gap surfaces — usually when a complex problem arrives and the engineer struggles — the drift has been underway for years. Early detection from ticket data can intervene at year 1 or 2, not year 5.

File Co-Change Network

Beyond individual engineer trajectories, the git history encodes the structural complexity of the codebase itself through file co-change patterns. Files that appear together in the same commit repeatedly are implicitly coupled — changes to one tend to require changes to the other.

We built a co-change graph from 23,539 multi-file commits across the three projects, producing 325,908 file-pair co-occurrence records. The top co-change pairs reveal both deliberate architectural coupling and maintenance patterns:

High co-change degree in a file is a predictor of ticket complexity: files appearing in the top 10% of co-change frequency were associated with tickets that resolved 2.8× slower on average and attracted 3.1× more comments. This makes co-change degree a useful feature for complexity classification that requires no manual annotation — it emerges entirely from commit history.

Engineers who regularly work on high-degree co-change files — the architectural hubs — show significantly different trajectory distributions: 18% Growing vs. 11% in the overall population, and 14% Coasting vs. 20% overall. Hub file exposure appears to be a structural driver of the escalation arc, likely because these files force broader cross-module awareness.

Implications

What This Means for Engineering Teams

The data suggests three actionable conclusions for engineering leaders:

1. The trajectory is readable years in advance

The coasting signal — declining complexity combined with rising resolution time — is detectable 2–3 years before it becomes a visible capability gap. Teams with access to ticket and commit data can identify at-risk engineers early enough to intervene with deliberate work assignment, not remediation.

2. Growth requires deliberate escalation, not just volume

The 70% who showed no trajectory were not less active than the Growing 11% — their ticket counts were comparable. The difference was the content of the work: growing engineers received progressively more demanding problems; the 70% received comparable problems on repeat. Volume of experience does not substitute for escalating challenge.

3. The signal is in data every company already has

This analysis required no surveys, no 360 reviews, no instrumentation beyond what any Jira + git setup already produces. Resolution dates, assignee fields, changed file lists, and module paths are sufficient inputs. The complexity map is latent in the commit history — it simply hasn't been read this way before.

The 70-20-10 assumption is a policy risk. If an organisation's talent development strategy relies on the 70% happening automatically, our data suggests roughly 80% of engineers will plateau or drift. The companies that treat work assignment as a deliberate learning instrument — using the signals already present in their engineering data — can shift those proportions. The curriculum already exists. The question is whether anyone is reading it.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations that affect generalisation:

Open-source context. Apache contributors are volunteers with variable levels of engagement. Career arcs may differ significantly from salaried engineers in commercial settings, where ticket assignment is more tightly controlled and team structures are more stable.

Anonymised identities. Apache Jira uses obfuscated author keys rather than names. We cannot confirm that the same key represents the same person across the full dataset, though keys are stable within the Jira system.

Complexity proxy. Our composite score captures breadth and core-centrality of code changes, not semantic difficulty. A one-line fix to a core file scores differently from a multi-file peripheral refactor. We have partially mitigated this by incorporating resolution time and watcher count as secondary signals, but the metric remains a proxy.

Small Growing cohort. With 42 engineers in the Growing category (11% of 378), subgroup analyses — such as hub-file exposure effects — are based on small samples and should be treated as directional, not definitive.
